# Paper Working Report

## 1. Project context

This scout pass is literature-first. The external literature already fixes two important facts for the thesis-extension problem. First, atomic-parity-violation (APV) theory and experiment are mature for heavy neutral atoms, especially Cs, and increasingly organized around nuclear weak-charge and neutron-skin systematics rather than only around raw signal size. Second, precision bound-electron `g`-factor work in highly charged ions is now at the level where subleading effects are discussed seriously, but the direct Standard-Model weak contribution to the bound-electron `g` factor is still a very thin literature niche compared with the QED, finite-size, and nuclear-structure literatures.

The broad question clarified by this search is therefore not "is weak interaction physics in atoms interesting?" but rather "which thesis-adjacent observable still has a defensible, narrow opening?" The literature suggests that a publishable extension is more likely to come from a careful bridge between weak neutral-current physics and the precision `g`-factor program, or from a very specifically chosen highly charged ion APV case, than from another broad Cs/Fr APV survey. As a support-only cross-check, `Reading/Thesisvf2.pdf` confirms that the thesis already contains preliminary weak `g`-factor estimates and APV benchmark calculations, so the external literature is being used here to decide what direction remains genuinely open rather than to restate the thesis.

## 2. Core literature

1. M.-A. Bouchiat and C. Bouchiat, "I. Parity violation induced by weak neutral currents in atomic physics," *J. Phys. France* **35**, 899-927 (1974). Source: https://doi.org/10.1051/jphys:019740035012089900 . Role in this project: foundational APV theory paper. Why it matters here: it is the classic paper that puts short-range neutral-current electron-nucleus interactions into an atomic-structure language and makes the heavy-atom enhancement argument explicit. Main claim/result: parity-violating neutral-current effects in atoms can be organized as short-range electron-nucleus potentials with strong heavy-atom enhancement, and alkali-like `s-p` mixing gives experimentally useful observables. Assumptions/approximation level: semi-analytic heavy-atom treatment with one-electron emphasis and pre-modern many-body precision. Direct usefulness here: it anchors the operator-level logic behind weak short-range potentials and explains why heavy atoms and heavy ions are the natural search space. What remains open: it does not tell us the modern size of correlation, neutron-density, or precision-observable systematics.

2. C. S. Wood, S. C. Bennett, D. Cho, B. P. Masterson, J. L. Roberts, C. E. Tanner, and C. E. Wieman, "Measurement of Parity Nonconservation and an Anapole Moment in Cesium," *Science* **275**, 1759-1763 (1997). Source: https://doi.org/10.1126/science.275.5307.1759 . Role in this project: benchmark low-energy weak-interaction experiment. Why it matters here: it remains the canonical APV reference point against which atomic weak calculations are judged. Main claim/result: the `6S-7S` cesium parity-nonconserving amplitude was measured precisely enough to test the Standard Model at low energy and to extract a nuclear-spin-dependent contribution associated with the anapole moment. Assumptions/approximation level: experiment plus atomic-structure interpretation through polarizabilities and many-body atomic theory. Direct usefulness here: any thesis extension that computes Stark-interference or weak-induced amplitudes needs a clear statement of how it sits relative to the Cs benchmark. What remains open: reproducing Cs alone is not a publishable gap; the literature around Cs is mature and crowded.

3. A. V. Viatkina, D. Antypas, M. G. Kozlov, D. Budker, and V. V. Flambaum, "Dependence of atomic parity-violation effects on neutron skins and new physics," *Phys. Rev. C* **100**, 034318 (2019). Source: https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevC.100.034318 . Role in this project: neutron-density and weak-charge sensitivity paper. Why it matters here: it shifts the APV conversation from pure amplitude enhancement toward nuclear weak-density control and isotope-pair design. Main claim/result: for a range of atoms including Cs and Fr, neutron skins and beyond-Standard-Model couplings enter APV observables in quantitatively comparable ways for some isotope strategies; the paper gives explicit coefficients and identifies favorable isotope choices. Assumptions/approximation level: spin-independent APV, Fermi proton/neutron density profiles, tree-level new-physics parametrization. Direct usefulness here: it is the cleanest literature support for treating neutron distributions and weak-charge densities as first-class inputs rather than small afterthoughts. What remains open: it does not solve the bound-electron `g`-factor problem, and it is not a full many-body APV treatment for specific highly charged ion transitions.

4. A. V. Viatkina, Ch. G. Mertens, B. Ohayon, V. A. Yerokhin, and A. Surzhykov, "Atomic parity violation in highly charged `^{40,48}Ca` and `^{208}Pb` ions," arXiv:2602.19962 (2026 preprint). Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.19962 . Role in this project: closest recent literature to the thesis-adjacent highly charged ion APV direction. Why it matters here: unlike the mature Cs/Fr literature, this is directly about H-like and Li-like ions and includes neutron-skin uncertainties. Main claim/result: the authors calculate APV-induced `E1` amplitudes for `1s -> 2s` and `1s^2 2s -> 1s^2 3s` transitions in Ca and Pb ions, finding that the Ca isotope pair largely suppresses neutron-skin contamination for some new-physics searches, whereas Pb keeps stronger neutron-skin and new-physics sensitivity. Assumptions/approximation level: spin-independent weak interaction, specific isotope set, preprint status, and an explicit focus on APV amplitudes rather than magnetic observables. Direct usefulness here: it shows that the HCI APV space is now active and that any new paper in that lane must be narrower, differently framed, or more diagnostic than a generic "APV in ions" calculation. What remains open: experimental feasibility, broader ion selection, and the connection to bound-electron `g` factors are still thin.

5. S. G. Karshenboim, R. N. Lee, and A. I. Milstein, "g factor of an electron or muon bound by an arbitrary central potential," *Phys. Rev. A* **72**, 042101 (2005). Source: https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevA.72.042101 . Role in this project: formal bridge from short-range central potentials to `g`-factor shifts. Why it matters here: this is the closest published theory paper to the diagonal weak-`g` problem if the weak interaction is organized as an additional short-range central potential in a one-electron Dirac problem. Main claim/result: the bound-particle `g` factor can be expressed in terms of binding-energy information for arbitrary central potentials, and non-Coulomb perturbations induce calculable `g`-factor shifts. Assumptions/approximation level: one-body Dirac theory with a central perturbing potential; not a weak-interaction paper and not a parity-mixing treatment. Direct usefulness here: it gives a literature-backed route for a parity-conserving weak correction to the bound-electron `g` factor without pretending such a route is already a standard APV result. What remains open: realistic proton/neutron weak densities, finite nuclear size, and parity-violating off-diagonal weak effects are outside the paper's scope.

Why it matters here: the weak `g`-factor idea is only interesting if it is stated against the actual strong-field precision frontier. Main claim/result: the `g` factor of hydrogen-like `^{118}Sn^{49+}` was measured at `0.5` ppb precision, enabling a strong-field QED test in a regime substantially beyond earlier low-`Z` benchmarks. Assumptions/approximation level: Penning-trap measurement in a heavy H-like ion, interpreted through state-of-the-art bound-state QED and nuclear corrections. Direct usefulness here: it fixes the real experimental scale that any weak `g`-factor estimate must be compared against. What remains open: the published sensitivity is still dominated by electromagnetic/QED and nuclear physics; it does not isolate a Standard-Model weak contribution.

Source: https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevLett.134.123001 . Role in this project: current high-`Z` theory floor for bound-electron `g` factors. Why it matters here: any weak-effect proposal must sit on top of the newest strong-field QED theory, not on an outdated uncertainty budget. Main claim/result: the last missing parts of the complete two-loop self-energy correction were computed exactly in `Z alpha`, improving the theory accuracy for hydrogen-like tin by about an order of magnitude and by a factor of `8` relative to the preceding state of the art. Assumptions/approximation level: all-order bound-state QED for H-like ions in strong fields, still within an electromagnetic and nuclear-corrections framework. Direct usefulness here: it shows that the precision `g`-factor platform is now limited by very specific high-order theory ingredients, so a weak correction paper must be framed as an additional subleading contribution on that exact footing. What remains open: it does not calculate weak neutral-current effects and therefore does not close the literature gap on the weak `g`-factor contribution itself.

## 3. Existing results relevant to this project

- The literature already establishes APV as a real, precision weak-interaction observable in heavy atoms. Bouchiat-Bouchiat gives the operator and scaling logic; Wood et al. gives the benchmark experimental realization. For a thesis extension, this means the question is no longer whether APV exists or whether heavy systems are favored; that part is settled.

- The literature also establishes that "weak charge" is not a purely particle-physics number once extracted from atoms. Papers such as Viatkina et al. (2019) show that neutron distributions, weak-density profiles, and isotope strategy enter the interpretation quantitatively. Any new paper that uses finite neutron densities is therefore working in a genuine literature-supported direction, not adding decorative nuclear realism.

- What seems only partially studied is the H-like / Li-like ion APV niche. The 2026 Viatkina et al. preprint shows this direction is now active and physically clean enough to be credible, but it is still much thinner than the Cs/Yb/Fr literature and remains explicitly tied to a small set of ions and transitions.

- On the `g`-factor side, what seems already established is not the weak correction itself but the precision environment in which such a correction would have to live. Karshenboim-Lee-Milstein gives a formal route for central-potential-induced `g` shifts, while the 2023-2025 tin papers show that strong-field `g`-factor measurement and theory are already precise enough that subleading effects matter as a bookkeeping question, even though Standard-Model weak effects are not yet standard entries in the published heavy-ion `g` tables.

- What seems not yet clearly resolved is whether the Standard-Model weak correction to the bound-electron `g` factor has a mature, dedicated literature comparable to APV. In this search, the answer is effectively no: the nearest literature is indirect, formal, or beyond-Standard-Model oriented. That is a real gap, but it is a gap in a very demanding precision landscape.

- Taken together, the project literature looks closest to a hybrid of a weak `g`-factor correction study and a weak-density-sensitivity study, with APV serving as the external benchmark and calibration literature. It looks less like a fresh Cs/Fr APV calculation paper and less like a broad "new physics with atoms" survey.

## 4. Literature-based constraints on the project

- A paper that only recalculates Cs or Fr APV amplitudes is not the realistic next step. The benchmark literature is too mature, and the novelty burden would be very high.

- A paper on highly charged ion APV is realistic only if it is tightly scoped. The 2026 Ca/Pb preprint means that a generic "APV in H-like/Li-like ions" paper is no longer an obvious opening; the system choice, observable choice, or uncertainty analysis would have to be different and clearly motivated.

- A direct weak bound-electron `g`-factor paper is literature-thin enough to be interesting, but it must be framed conservatively. The recent tin `g`-factor literature shows that the relevant comparison class is not a rough order-of-magnitude estimate; it is a precision budget dominated by QED, finite-size, and nuclear inputs.

- The support-only thesis cross-check sharpens this constraint. `Reading/Thesisvf2.pdf` already quotes a preliminary weak `1s` `g`-factor correction of order `10^-12` for a very heavy ion, whereas the recent published high-`Z` `g`-factor measurements and theory are still substantially above that scale in absolute uncertainty. This does not kill the direction, but it does mean the realistic paper claim is "size, scaling, and limiting uncertainties of the weak correction," not "near-term direct detection."

- Any viable paper in this space must therefore keep proton and neutron density modeling explicit. The neutron-skin/APV literature makes it clear that weak-density assumptions are physically meaningful, and the heavy-ion `g`-factor literature makes it clear that nuclear-structure uncertainties cannot be hidden under a generic "small correction" label.

- The most defensible thesis-adjacent direction is one where a negative or limiting result is still valuable. In other words, if the literature-driven conclusion is that the Standard-Model weak `g` correction is far below current sensitivity but calculable in a controlled way, that can still be a coherent paper outcome. The literature does not support overselling near-term observability.

## 5. Possible narrow paper angle from literature

The most realistic narrow paper angle suggested by the literature is a short, careful theory paper on the **spin-independent weak neutral-current correction to the bound-electron `g` factor in a very small set of heavy H-like ions**, treated as a precision-limit and scaling study rather than as a discovery proposal. The paper would compute the weak contribution with explicit finite proton and neutron densities, compare it against the current dominant QED and nuclear uncertainty floor from the recent heavy-ion `g`-factor literature, and use APV/neutron-skin papers only as external support for the weak-charge-density inputs and for the interpretation of neutron-distribution sensitivity.

This angle is cautious but realistic for three reasons. First, the APV literature is already mature enough that repeating standard heavy-atom amplitude work is unlikely to be the best thesis extension. Second, the direct weak `g`-factor literature is still genuinely thin, so a rigorous Standard-Model estimate is not obviously redundant. Third, the existing thesis already contains the right physical ingredients for such a bridge, while the literature strongly warns against claiming detectability before the comparison to QED and nuclear systematics is done explicitly.

## 6. Candidate paper directions
- **Rank 1: spin-independent weak correction to the bound-electron `g` factor in a very small set of heavy H-like ions.** Strengths: this is the closest match to the thesis, the direct weak-`g` literature is still thin, and the one-electron H-like setting avoids the many-body-correlation overhead that makes a fresh APV paper harder to place. Weaknesses: the signal is expected to be tiny relative to current QED, finite-size, and nuclear-structure uncertainty floors, so the paper must be framed as a scale-comparison / feasibility study rather than as a near-term precision extraction claim.
- **Rank 2: tightly scoped H-like / Li-like APV with explicit neutron-density systematics.** Strengths: the weak-density physics is directly literature-supported, and the repository already contains weak radial kernels and amplitude machinery. Weaknesses: the 2026 Ca/Pb HCI APV preprint means this lane is now active, so a generic APV calculation would look late unless the system choice or uncertainty strategy is unusually sharp; it is also more exposed to basis-completeness and many-body-interpretation questions than the H-like `g` route.
- **Rank 3: comparative weak-density sensitivity paper linking APV amplitudes and weak `g`-factor shifts.** Strengths: scientifically attractive and thesis-coherent because it connects the same weak source to two observables. Weaknesses: for one paper it is probably too broad, risks becoming half review and half pilot study, and would dilute the cleanest publishable result.
## 7. Recommended main goal
The recommended main goal is a compact theory paper on the **spin-independent Standard-Model weak correction to the ground-state bound-electron `g` factor in a small set of heavy H-like ions**, with explicit finite proton and neutron densities and a direct comparison to the present QED / nuclear uncertainty floor. The main deliverable is not a claim of observability; it is a controlled statement of size, `Z`-scaling, neutron-density sensitivity, and present detectability limits.

For this paper, the bound-electron `g` factor is the **main focus**. APV remains supporting context only, mainly to justify the weak-density modeling and to position the result relative to the broader weak-interaction literature.
## 8. Why this is the best extension of the thesis
This is the strongest thesis extension because it grows directly out of the repository's existing weak-potential and `g`-factor directions while still occupying a real literature gap. It uses the thesis as a starting point rather than repeating the mature Cs benchmark literature or chasing a now-active HCI APV lane where the novelty burden has already increased.

It is better than the APV alternative because H-like `g` factors are physically cleaner: one electron, no large many-body correlation program, and a clearer separation between the target weak effect and the repository's existing numerical assets. It is better than the hybrid APV-plus-`g` option because a narrow negative-or-limiting result on weak `g` shifts is still publishable, whereas a split-focus paper would be harder to finish cleanly and harder to sell as one main result.
## 9. System and observable definition
- **Primary systems:** heavy H-like ions only, with a final set kept very small. A practical target set is one current precision anchor plus one or two higher-`Z` amplifiers, e.g. `^{118}Sn^{49+}`, `^{208}Pb^{81+}`, and `^{238}U^{91+}`. `Sn` anchors the comparison to the present precision `g`-factor literature; `Pb` and `U` test the stronger short-range weak scaling.
- **State:** the ground `1s` bound-electron state.
- **Primary observable:** the spin-independent weak neutral-current correction `delta g_W` to the bound-electron ground-state `g` factor.
- **Secondary diagnostic observable:** sensitivity of `delta g_W` to the neutron-density model or neutron radius, reported as a controlled variation rather than as a full isotope-chain program.
- **Why this is clean:** the H-like system suppresses electron-correlation ambiguity, keeps the operator structure close to a one-body Dirac problem, and lets the weak effect be compared directly with known QED, finite-size, recoil, and nuclear-structure backgrounds.
## 10. Central theory direction
At the conceptual level the paper should start from a one-electron Dirac Hamiltonian for a finite nucleus in an external magnetic field, with separate pieces for the Coulomb-plus-finite-size binding potential, the magnetic interaction defining `g`, and the weak neutral-current perturbation. The weak term should be split explicitly into the spin-independent parity-conserving central source, which is the natural entry point for the main `delta g_W` calculation, and the parity-violating off-diagonal term, which is relevant background context but should not be the main observable in this paper.

The perturbative structure should be linear in the weak interaction and linear in the magnetic interaction. In practice the paper direction is: compute the weak-induced shift to the bound-state `g` factor for a central short-range perturbation, with finite proton and neutron densities carried explicitly in the weak source, then compare that shift against the standard precision-budget entries. The radial ingredients are therefore finite-density weak source integrals, the unperturbed H-like Dirac wavefunctions, and a controlled mapping from the central weak perturbation to `delta g`; APV-style off-diagonal `s-p_{1/2}` mixing should remain a supporting comparison, not the main derivation target.
## 11. Main claim the paper could make
The paper can plausibly claim that the spin-independent Standard-Model weak contribution to the bound-electron `g` factor in heavy H-like ions is a well-defined and computable subleading correction with explicit neutron-density dependence, but that it remains below the current experimental-theory precision floor by a substantial margin. That is still a useful result because it turns the weak term from a vague omission into a quantified entry in the high-`Z` precision hierarchy.
## 12. Backgrounds and scale competition
- **Finite nuclear size and neutron distributions:** these are the nearest structural competitors because the weak term is also short-range and nucleus-centered. They must be treated as part of the comparison, not as an afterthought.
- **QED:** one-loop and especially recent two-loop bound-state QED corrections set the real theory floor in high-`Z` `g` factors; any weak result has to be stated relative to that literature, not against a bare Dirac baseline.
- **Recoil and nuclear polarization:** likely subleading to the main QED / finite-size budget in the heaviest ions, but still part of the precision bookkeeping and potentially comparable to or larger than the weak term.
- **Experiment:** the recent heavy-ion `g`-factor measurements are extraordinarily precise, but the Scout literature already indicates that the expected weak correction is still well below current absolute sensitivity.
- **Assessment:** this makes the project strongest as a **scale-comparison / feasibility paper**, not as a bold standalone precision claim. If the final numbers stay near the thesis-level `10^-12` scale for very heavy ions, the honest conclusion will be that the weak correction is physically real and systematically characterizable, but not yet experimentally separable.
## 13. Minimal publishable calculation
The minimal publishable result is a three-part calculation for a very small H-like set: `(i)` tabulate `delta g_W(1s)` with explicit finite proton and neutron densities, `(ii)` show the dominant `Z` and neutron-density scaling across the set, and `(iii)` place the result beside the current QED / finite-size / recoil uncertainty hierarchy from the literature. One compact sensitivity study with a changed neutron radius or neutron skin is enough to make the nuclear-input dependence concrete.

That would already improve on the thesis if it goes beyond a preliminary order-of-magnitude estimate and produces a literature-positioned, uncertainty-aware weak `g`-factor benchmark. A Li-like extension should be treated as optional follow-on work, not as part of the minimal paper.
## 14. What is already present in the repository
- The thesis already appears to contain preliminary weak `g`-factor estimates and APV benchmark material, so the physics motivation is not starting from zero.
- The copied repository contains explicit weak-potential infrastructure with finite proton/neutron inputs in the `rad` / `rad_f2py` line, which is enough to anchor the weak-density side of the project.
- The copied repository also contains bound-electron `g`-factor program families for H-like, Li-like, and boron-like systems, plus direct Python-facing access to the lithium `g` suite and executable bridges for the broader copied workspace.
- The repo therefore already has the two needed pillars in separate form: weak nuclear source handling and high-`Z` `g`-factor machinery. What is missing is the controlled paper-level bridge between them for the H-like weak `g` correction.
## 15. What is missing
- A paper-safe derivation of `delta g_W` in the repository's conventions, including the precise role of the central weak potential in the bound-state `g`-factor formalism.
- A convention check for the weak source itself before any production numerics are trusted, especially where parity-conserving / parity-violating usage and weak-coupling constants differ across copied and protected paths.
- A final system choice and nuclear-input protocol: which H-like ions, which proton/neutron density model, and what neutron-radius variation is used for the sensitivity estimate.
- A direct numerical path that combines the weak source with the H-like `g`-factor calculation rather than leaving them as separate program families.
- A compact comparison table against the modern literature uncertainty budget so the paper can state clearly whether the weak term is negligible, limiting, or potentially relevant.
## 16. Risk flags
- The main physical risk is that `delta g_W` is so far below the present QED / finite-size / nuclear-structure floor that the paper reads as "known to be negligible" rather than as a useful quantified result.
- The main numerical risk is that the weak-potential convention issues already flagged by Scout could contaminate the normalization or interpretation of the weak source if they are not resolved first.
- The main scope risk is drifting from a narrow H-like feasibility paper into a broader APV or multi-charge-state project that is harder to finish and easier to challenge.
- The main publication risk is overclaiming observability. The safer and more honest positioning is that the weak bound-electron `g` factor is presently a **subleading but quantifiable Standard-Model correction**, not a near-term precision-discovery channel.
- A secondary risk is that the neutron-density sensitivity may be too modest to add much beyond the basic scale estimate; if so, the paper should stay centered on the weak-correction benchmark itself rather than forcing a larger nuclear-structure claim.

## 17. Reviewer comments – pass 1

### 1. Executive verdict
**Verdict: interesting but presently too weak.** The Builder direction is not nonsense, but it is not yet clean enough to carry a paper in its current form. The only version that looks defensible is a very narrow H-like benchmark paper on the spin-independent **parity-conserving central** weak contribution to `delta g`, with no claim of observability and no attempt to sell neutron-density sensitivity as a coequal result. Right now the report still overstates the cleanliness of the observable: the short-range weak source and the dominant nuclear backgrounds live in the same radial region, and the formal definition of `delta g_W` has not been pinned down tightly enough. The novelty is real only in a narrow sense: there is a thin literature niche, not a clearly established missing precision term that the community is already waiting for. A referee could accept this only as a compact feasibility / hierarchy paper. A referee should reject it if it drifts into a broader "new weak probe in heavy ions" claim.

### 2. Paper-strength assessment
As a paper, the current proposal is borderline. The central observable is only conditionally clean: it is clean **if and only if** the paper commits to one sharply defined quantity, namely the `1s` H-like `g`-factor shift induced by a spin-independent central weak potential at first order in the weak interaction and first order in the magnetic interaction. The physics claim is moderately interesting because the direct Standard-Model weak-`g` literature is sparse, but sparsity alone is not enough; the paper must convert that sparsity into a controlled benchmark that is actually useful. The present scope is still slightly too broad because it tries to keep neutron-density sensitivity and APV context too close to the main claim. It meaningfully improves the thesis only if it replaces a preliminary estimate with a convention-clean, literature-positioned benchmark and a hard comparison to the present uncertainty floor. A good referee is unlikely to view the current framing as publishable without further narrowing; it still reads too much like a thesis continuation with a better comparison table.

### 3. Physics audit
The Builder plan is physically viable only for the **parity-conserving diagonal weak source**, not for a blurred mixture of APV logic and `g`-factor logic. For the proposed `1s` H-like observable, the main paper cannot lean on the usual parity-mixing story that motivates APV amplitudes; that is a different observable class. The relevant physical picture here is much duller but cleaner: a tiny nucleus-localized Standard-Model central perturbation modifies the magnetic response of a one-electron bound state. That is acceptable physics, but it is not a "clean weak signature" in the experimental sense because the same short-distance region is already crowded by finite-size and nuclear-structure effects. The H-like choice is the correct one if this project moves forward at all; Li-like would immediately weaken the paper by adding correlation and screening questions before the central observable is even secured. The neutron-density dependence is physically real, but it is probably not strong enough to be a main selling point: for an absolute `delta g_W`, it is more likely to be a fractional modulation of an already buried correction than a qualitatively new sensitivity channel. The bound-electron `g` factor is therefore viable only as a narrow **main result of benchmark type**, not as a strong discovery-style or precision-extraction target.

### 4. Mathematical audit
The report still under-specifies the observable mathematically. It must state exactly what `delta g_W` means: either the derivative of the weak-corrected bound-state energy with respect to the external magnetic field at `B = 0`, or an explicitly equivalent central-potential formula tied to the Karshenboim-Lee-Milstein framework. Without that, "weak correction to `g`" is only a verbal label. The order counting must also be fixed explicitly: first order in the weak interaction, first order in the magnetic interaction, with no hidden double counting between the unperturbed finite-size potential and the weak central source. The paper must also prove, not assume, that the parity-violating off-diagonal weak term is outside the chosen observable at the working order, or else the central-only reduction is not mathematically closed. The current report further inherits a serious convention problem from Scout: the copied and protected weak-source paths are not convention-identical, and the `Cv_e` / `Cv_p` definitions do not presently look paper-safe. Until those conventions are reconciled, the sign, normalization, and even the physical interpretation of `delta g_W` remain under-specified.

### 5. Scale and background audit
The scale hierarchy is the main problem, and the report should say that more brutally. The thesis-level estimate already quoted in sections 1-5 places the weak `1s` correction near `10^-12` for a very heavy ion, while the present high-`Z` `g`-factor experimental/theory environment discussed by Scout is still at or above the `10^-9` absolute level. That is already a gap of at least a few orders of magnitude before adding any uncertainty from nuclear modeling. Finite nuclear size is the closest structural competitor and is much more dangerous here than the Builder text suggests: it is not merely "another background," it probes the same nucleus-centered short-range region and is vastly larger. Recoil and nuclear-polarization terms are also likely to sit above the weak piece in absolute size, even if they are not the dominant published floor in every ion. The neutron-density dependence of `delta g_W` may be interesting fractionally, but that does not rescue the paper experimentally; it mainly changes the interpretation of a correction that is already too small to isolate. This project therefore does **not** support a precision-prediction framing. It supports, at best, a compact scale-comparison / feasibility paper.

### 6. Required changes before Simulation
**Must-fix issues**
- Freeze the observable to one quantity: the spin-independent, parity-conserving central contribution to `delta g_W(1s)` in H-like ions.
- Write a paper-safe derivation of that observable in the repository's conventions before any numerical plan is proposed.
- Resolve the weak-source convention mismatch flagged by Scout, including the `Cv_e` / `Cv_p` issue and the copied-vs-protected parity split.
- State the expected magnitude against the current QED / FNS / nuclear floor immediately and explicitly, so the project stops pretending the scale separation might be favorable.
- Stop treating neutron-density sensitivity as a main claim unless a differential observable is identified that amplifies it meaningfully.

**Should-fix issues**
- Reduce the ion list to at most two serious cases: one literature anchor and one high-`Z` amplifier.
- Move APV discussion out of the main result and keep it as short motivation or comparison only.
- Make clear that the current H-like `g` code path is not already a weak-`g` workflow; it currently computes the ordinary Dirac `g` factor for a finite nuclear potential, so a formal bridge is still missing.

**Optional upgrades**
- Add one compact scaling argument or diagnostic ratio only if it clarifies the hierarchy without pretending to improve observability.

### 7. Recommended stronger framing
**Rank 1:** a compact benchmark paper titled in substance as "spin-independent Standard-Model weak contribution to the `1s` bound-electron `g` factor in heavy H-like ions: size, scaling, and comparison to current theory and experimental floors." This is the strongest framing because it says exactly what the work is and does not oversell it.

**Rank 2:** an even narrower methods-oriented paper on the central weak contribution to H-like `g` factors with finite proton/neutron densities, where neutron-density dependence is presented only as an uncertainty/sensitivity band. This is less exciting but probably more referee-proof.

**Rank 3:** a broader weak-density comparison paper linking APV and `g` shifts. This is scientifically appealing but presently the weakest publication strategy because it spreads the story across two observables before either one is nailed down tightly enough.

### 8. Minimum defensible next step

### 17.9 Follow-up review: H-like / Li-like specific-difference option
**Verdict: keep this only as a later extension, or at most as a short exploratory subsection; do not promote it to a central paper component.** The specific-difference construction is physically motivated for suppressing leading FNS contributions, but that is exactly why it is a poor default strategy for the weak correction: the spin-independent weak source is also a short-range, nucleus-centered effect, so the same cancellation logic is likely to remove a substantial fraction of the weak signal along with the FNS background. This route is therefore not a clean "reveal the weak effect" strategy. At best it is a diagnostic test of whether a weak residual survives an FNS-tuned cancellation. That is a much narrower and less glamorous claim.

The main physics reason for skepticism is structural similarity. For `ns` states in heavy ions, both the FNS shift and the spin-independent weak contribution are dominated by the wavefunction in the nuclear region, and both are driven by central short-range sources. They are not identical operators, because the weak source carries proton/neutron weighting rather than pure charge weighting, and the Li-like state brings screening and state-composition differences, but the first expectation should still be **partial cancellation of the weak term**, not enhancement. If the paper wants to argue otherwise, it would need an explicit radial and scaling analysis rather than analogy.

The main mathematical concern is that `delta g'_W = delta g_W^Li - xi delta g_W^H` is not automatically a clean observable just because `g' = g_Li - xi g_H` is standard for FNS suppression. The coefficient `xi` is chosen to cancel the leading FNS piece in the ordinary `g` factor, not to isolate weak physics. Once Li-like screening, interelectronic interaction, and correlation corrections enter, the weak residual becomes strongly `xi`-construction-dependent and potentially mixed with many-body terms that are harder to define consistently than the H-like benchmark itself. Before the H-like `delta g_W` is formally secured, adding a Li-like specific difference risks building a second-layer derived observable on top of a first-layer observable that is not yet fully pinned down.

The main scale and complexity concern is that this likely moves the project in the wrong direction. Even if the weak residual does not cancel exactly, it is more likely to become **smaller** in absolute size than the already tiny H-like `delta g_W`, while the Li-like side adds screening, correlation, effective-potential choices, and a more demanding validation burden. That is a bad trade at the current project stage. It exchanges a clean one-electron benchmark for a multi-electron cancellation problem before the basic weak normalization, sign convention, and H-like scale estimate are fully trusted.

**Recommendation:** do not reject the idea permanently, but keep it out of the main paper plan for now. The honest publishable use is a short exploratory section, or better a later follow-on project, framed as: *test whether the Standard-Model weak contribution survives an FNS-suppressing H-like / Li-like specific-difference construction*. It should not be sold as a method to expose the weak effect. The baseline paper should remain H-like only; revisit the specific-difference option only after the plain H-like `delta g_W(1s)` observable, conventions, and hierarchy comparison are fully established.

### 18.2 Final observable definition
The target quantity is the weak correction to the Zeeman `g` factor of the H-like `1s_{1/2}` state generated by the **spin-independent, parity-conserving central weak source** only. A paper-safe definition is

```math
\delta g_W(1s) \equiv g\big[ V_{\mathrm{C+FNS}} + V_W^{\mathrm{pc}} \big] - g\big[ V_{\mathrm{C+FNS}} \big]
```

evaluated to first order in `V_W^{pc}` and to first order in the external magnetic field. For the actual computational pathway of this project, however, `\delta g_W(1s)` is to be obtained from the central-potential mass-derivative identity of Karshenboim-Lee-Milstein type applied to the diagonal weak energy shift, not from a direct Zeeman-response computation as the primary working route. The working formula is

```math
\delta g = - \frac{\kappa}{2 j (j+1)}
\left[\frac{2\kappa}{c^2}\frac{\partial \Delta E}{\partial m_e}\right]
= - \frac{\kappa^2}{j(j+1)c^2}\frac{\partial \Delta E}{\partial m_e}.
```

For the H-like `1s_{1/2}` state, with `\kappa = -1` and `j = 1/2`, this becomes

```math
\delta g_W(1s) = - \frac{4}{3c^2}\frac{\partial \Delta E_W(1s)}{\partial m_e}.
```

The generic Zeeman-energy definition may still be retained as a formal definition of `g`, but the actual computational route for this project is the explicit central-potential identity above, with the overall sign still checked against the repository's magnetic-moment and charge conventions.

In this project, "`delta g_W`" therefore does **not** mean a generic weak correction including parity-violating `s-p_{1/2}` mixing, hyperfine-dependent weak terms, or Li-like specific differences. It means one sharply defined mixed magnetic-plus-central-weak response of the H-like `1s` state.

That is less dramatic than APV, but it is also the cleanest route that survives the Reviewer's criticism.

The Hamiltonian should be organized schematically as

```math
H = H_D + V_{\mathrm{C+FNS}} + V_B + V_W^{\mathrm{pc}} + V_W^{\mathrm{pv}},
```

where the baseline paper keeps only terms linear in `V_B` and linear in `V_W^{pc}`. ### 18.4 Key formulas and scaling anchors
The numerical object that Simulation must ultimately control is the diagonal weak energy shift for the H-like `1s` state,

```math
\Delta E_W(a) = \langle a | V_W^{\mathrm{pc}} | a \rangle.
```

For the diagonal `1s` problem this is schematically

```math
\Delta E_W(1s) = \int_0^\infty dr \, \big[g_{1s}^2(r) + f_{1s}^2(r)\big] \, V_W^{\mathrm{pc}}(r),
```

with support concentrated in the nuclear region. The precise normalization of `V_W^{pc}` must remain explicit until the weak-source conventions are reconciled.

The `g`-factor correction is then to be obtained through the central-potential identity

```math
\delta g = - \frac{\kappa^2}{j(j+1)c^2}\frac{\partial \Delta E}{\partial m_e},
```

and therefore for the H-like `1s` state,

```math
\delta g_W(1s) = - \frac{4}{3c^2}\frac{\partial \Delta E_W(1s)}{\partial m_e}.
```

This is the central formula-level anchor for the project. Two scaling anchors matter immediately:

```math
\lambda = \sqrt{m_e^2 - E_a^2},
```

which controls the bound-state radial scale, and the finite-nucleus size parameter,

```math
m_e R_N
```

in relativistic units. The mass derivative must be interpreted carefully: it is taken at fixed **physical** proton and neutron densities, fixed weak couplings, and fixed weak-source normalization, not at fixed dimensionless code inputs. It therefore acts through the bound-state scale `\lambda`, through the energy dependence hidden inside `\lambda`, and through the finite-nucleus parameter `m_e R_N`. Simulation does not need the full derivation here, but it does need to know that `\delta g_W` is a derivative-sensitive finite-nucleus quantity derived from `\Delta E_W(1s)` by a specific central-potential identity, not by multiplying `\Delta E_W` by an unspecified constant.

Numerical finite-difference route with explicit mass insertion.` Plausible but not yet confirmed: this is the conceptually cleanest route if `m_e` is made an explicit solver parameter and the `1s` state is regenerated for `m_e (1 \pm \delta)`. Correctness: high in principle. Implementation difficulty: high, because `bspqed_dkb::solve` hard-wires the unit-mass Dirac structure and the current `bspqed` hydrogenic programs do not accept `m_e` as runtime input. Hidden-unit risk: medium once implemented, but the code changes required to get there are broad enough that trust would be lost unless the rewrite were very carefully audited. Compatibility with the current copied tree: poor for a first implementation pass. Fixed-physical-density requirement: good in principle, because the physical inputs can be held directly. Verdict: too invasive for the first trustworthy implementation.
- `C. For each trial mass, rebuild the code-level inputs from fixed physical inputs: convert the physical nuclear radius to the trial `rnucl(m_e)`, rebuild the weak normalization consistently from fixed physical `G_F`, `Z`, `N`, and the chosen density model, resolve the `1s` state with the unchanged DKB solver, and compute `\Delta E_W(1s)` directly from the coefficients. Correctness: high enough for the intended benchmark if the unit map is frozen first. Implementation difficulty: moderate. Hidden-unit risk: moderate but auditable. Compatibility with the current `bspqed` / H-like `g` stack: good, because the solver and the existing `g` kernel stay intact. Fixed-physical-density requirement: good, because the wrapper can treat fm inputs as primary and recompute all dimensionless quantities from them. Verdict: the safest practical route for this project.

### 19.6 Recommended method and why
- Confirmed from code inspection: the present repository is **implementable with modest extension only after narrowing and convention freeze**. The right numerical center is the H-like DKB stack, not the sampled `rad` kernel path by itself.
- Recommended method: use the hybrid controlled-rescaling route, but anchor it to a new direct H-like weak-energy driver built from existing kernels, not to sampled interpolation. The direct future computation should be:
  solve the H-like finite-size Coulomb problem with `bspqed_dkb::solve`,
  evaluate `\Delta E_W(1s)` with `bspqed_integrals::intlw` and a convention-safe weak central potential,
  and then probe `\partial \Delta E_W / \partial m_e` by symmetric finite differences in a wrapper that reconstructs the code inputs from fixed physical fm inputs and fixed physical weak couplings.
- Why this is preferred: it keeps the validated DKB and hydrogenic `g` kernels intact, exposes the mass dependence without rewriting the solver, and keeps the weak-energy functional and the later `g`-factor extraction on the same one-electron basis.
- Why the sampled `rad` path is not recommended as the primary production route: it is currently interpolation-based, disconnected from the H-like `g` executable family, and tied to the copied single-radius weak source. It is still valuable as a support-path cross-check.
- Blocker / unresolved convention issue: the Karshenboim-Lee-Milstein sign/prefactor cannot be used operationally until it is matched explicitly to the repository's H-like `g` sign convention. The code inspection in this pass does not settle that.
- Blocker / unresolved convention issue: section 18 assumes explicit proton and neutron densities, but the copied weak source does not yet provide them. The recommended route therefore has two phases: an internal common-radius shakedown and a production phase after the weak-density model is upgraded.

### 20.2 Numerical risk audit
- `1. Highest risk:` weak-source convention mismatch. The copied/protected trees are not convention-identical for off-diagonal weak usage, and the copied `Cv_e` / `Cv_p` comments do not match the implemented formulas. If this is not fixed, every later number risks a wrong sign or normalization.
- `2. Highest risk:` the present weak source is not yet an explicit proton-density / neutron-density model. A one-radius hard sphere is not the same physics object as the section-18 baseline paper claim.
- `3. High risk:` hidden `m_e` dependence. The current code absorbs `m_e` into the solver, the radius conversion, and `Gfermi_relativistic`. A derivative taken at fixed dimensionless inputs would be physically wrong even if numerically stable.
- `4. High risk:` magnetic sign convention opacity. The H-like `g` kernel encodes the sign through `linear_potential`, `vfunc`, and the `gfactor` prefactor rather than through explicit `e` and `\mu_B` definitions. That is fine internally and dangerous for a new derivative identity unless audited carefully.
- `5. Medium risk:` sampled-`rad` interpolation error and cross-family drift. The sampled weak route uses linear interpolation and a different workflow from the H-like `g` solver. It is useful for cross-checks and should not be silently promoted to the production route.
- `6. Medium risk:` subtraction noise in the mass derivative. `\Delta E_W` is tiny; the derivative can be much noisier than the integral itself if the finite-difference step or convergence settings are not chosen carefully.
- `7. Medium risk:` basis and cavity sensitivity masked by cancellation. The same weak term is localized at the nucleus while the derivative is extracted by subtraction. Convergence must be tighter than for ordinary unperturbed `g` values.
- `8. Lower but real risk:` reproducibility drift between `bspqed` and `bspwf` constants. The copied repository uses two nearby but non-identical CODATA sets, so mixed-path comparisons must document which constant set is active.

## 22. Python first-order weak-shift wrapper

### 22.1 Purpose
`workspace_f2py/First_order.py` is the first thin Python orchestration layer for the existing sampled-rad first-order weak-energy correction. It does not re-derive or re-implement the parity-conserving weak integral in Python; it loads sampled `r, G, F` data, imports the compiled `f2py` module, calls the wrapped Fortran routine, and prints the resulting `\Delta E_W`.

### 22.2 Relation to `something.f90`
`workspace_f2py/programs/rad/src/something.f90` already contains the sampled first-order weak-energy correction logic through `run_first_order_energy_correction`, which reads sampled radial data, builds the same one-radius weak potential, and integrates `(G^2 + F^2) V_W^{pc}(r)` from `0` to `R`. `workspace_f2py/First_order.py` exposes that same sampled-rad route from Python through the copied `f2py` kernel in `workspace_f2py/programs/rad_f2py/src/rad_kernel_f2py.f90`, without adding a new Fortran driver or altering the existing solver logic.

### 22.3 Imported `f2py` module
The wrapper imports the compiled module `rad_kernel_suite`, using `workspace_f2py_api.load_rad_kernel_suite_module()` when available and falling back to a direct import from `workspace_f2py/programs/rad_f2py` if needed. The exact wrapped Fortran routine called is `rad_kernel_suite.rad_kernel_f2py.first_order_weak_shift`, with the discovered `f2py` signature

```text
e_pot, r_rel, ierr = first_order_weak_shift(znucl, nneutrons, r_fm, r, g, f)
```

This was confirmed by live Python introspection in the current environment rather than assumed from placeholder documentation.

### 22.4 Input data
The calculation requires a sampled radial wavefunction file containing `r`, `G(r)`, and `F(r)` columns in the relativistic normalization used by the copied `rad` workflow. Spectrum files or basis-coefficient exports are not enough by themselves, because the present wrapped kernel expects already sampled radial functions on a monotonic grid.

The wrapper now also checks the sampled normalization `\int (G^2 + F^2) dr`. If the file is badly non-unit-normalized, it stops by default rather than silently scaling `\Delta E_W`. This is important because the legacy reference file `workspace_f2py/programs/rad/data/reference/radial_functions/radial_function_relunits.txt`, still used by the old `something.f90` first-order energy path, is not unit-normalized and should only be used for explicit legacy-path reproduction.

The wrapper accepts either:
- `--radius-fm` for the current one-radius sampled weak kernel used by `something.f90`
- `--rms-fm`, which is converted in Python to the current common-sphere radius through `R = sqrt(5/3) * rms`

That conversion is only a bridge into the present one-radius weak model; it is not yet a new proton-density / neutron-density upgrade.

### 22.5 Command-line interface
The script now lives inside `workspace_f2py/` so it can be run directly as

```bash

```

On success it reports the import route, imported module name, exact Fortran routine called, `Z`, `N`, the fm input used, the number of sampled points, `r` range, a sampled normalization check `\int (G^2 + F^2) dr`, and the returned `\Delta E_W`.

## 24. Mass-derivative prototype for weak g-factor correction

### 24.1 Purpose
This pass builds the first mass-derivative prototype for the weak correction to the H-like bound-electron `g` factor. The route used here is the virial / Karshenboim-Lee-Milstein mass derivative of the sampled-rad weak energy shift, not the direct magnetic-response `g_uehling` route.

The goal is not yet a final paper-safe `\delta g_W`, but a controlled orchestration layer that regenerates sampled `1s` wavefunctions, evaluates the already validated sampled weak-energy kernel, and forms the finite-difference derivative.

### 24.2 Formula
The working formula is

```text
delta_g_W = kappa^2 / [j (j + 1)] * d(Delta E_W) / d m_e
```

For the `1s` state, `kappa = -1` and `j = 1/2`, so

```text
delta_g_W(1s) = 4/3 * d(Delta E_W) / d m_e
```

The prototype evaluates the weak energy shift in relativistic code units at `mu = m_e / m_e0` and then forms

```text
D_W = [ (1 + h) Delta_epsilon_W(1 + h) - (1 - h) Delta_epsilon_W(1 - h) ] / (2 h)
delta_g_W(1s) = (4 / 3) * D_W
```

### 24.3 Mass scaling convention
This prototype keeps the physical inputs fixed:

- fixed `Z`
- fixed `N`
- fixed physical nuclear radius in fm
- fixed physical weak coupling `G_F`

The code-level mass scaling is implemented as follows:

- `R_code(mu) = mu * R_code(1)`
- `G_F_code(mu) = mu^2 * G_F_code(1)`

In the copied tree, the current sampled weak kernel does not accept `G_F_code` as an explicit input. Inspection of `workspace_f2py/programs/rad_f2py/src/rad_kernel_f2py.f90` and `workspace_f2py/codes/bspqed/src/bspqed_potentials.f90` shows that the weak shift is linear in the built-in `Gfermi_relativistic`, so this prototype applies the missing `mu^2` factor explicitly on the Python side:

```text
Delta_epsilon_W(mu) = mu^2 * [sampled f2py weak shift returned at that mu-scaled radius and wavefunction]
```

For the radius scaling:

- `workspace_f2py/programs/rad/src/practise.f90` accepts a nuclear RMS radius in fm for H-like wavefunction generation
- the current weak sampled kernel uses the common sphere radius `R` in fm

Therefore the wrapper keeps a physical fm input and passes the effective code-level fm inputs

- `rms_fm_effective = mu * rms_fm_physical`
- `radius_fm_effective = mu * radius_fm_physical`

when the intended fixed-physical-radius derivative is requested.

### 24.4 Numerical path
The new script is `workspace_f2py/Mass_derivative.py`.

Its workflow is:

```text
mu
 -> regenerate sampled H-like 1s wavefunction with practise.f90
 -> archive the sampled r, G, F table
 -> call the validated f2py sampled weak-shift kernel
 -> multiply the returned weak shift by mu^2 to enforce fixed physical G_F
 -> form mu * Delta_epsilon_W(mu)
 -> central finite difference D_W
 -> delta_g_W(1s) = (4/3) * D_W
```

The default mode regenerates the `1s` wavefunction for each `mu`. The wrapper also contains two explicit controls:

- `--wavefunction-mode reuse`: reuses one sampled `1s` wavefunction for all `mu` values and prints a warning that this is incomplete
- `--radius-scaling-mode fixed-code`: keeps the code radius fixed and prints a warning that this is the incorrect derivative convention

## 26. Weak-source convention freeze

### 26.1 Purpose

### 26.2 Active code path
The active sampled-rad path is:

```text
workspace_f2py/Mass_derivative.py or workspace_f2py/First_order.py
-> workspace_f2py/programs/rad_f2py/src/rad_kernel_f2py.f90
-> workspace_f2py/codes/bspqed/src/bspqed_potentials.f90
-> sampled integral for Delta E_W
```

`First_order.py` resolves `--rms-fm` to a common sphere radius `R_fm = sqrt(5/3) rms_fm`, reads sampled `r, G, F`, and calls `first_order_weak_shift(Z, N, R_fm, r, G, F)`. The f2py kernel converts `R_fm` to `R_rel`, initializes `type(weak_potential)`, and integrates

```text
Delta E_W = integral_0^R_rel dr (G(r)^2 + F(r)^2) V_W^pc(r).
```

`Mass_derivative.py` uses the same first-order sampled weak-shift kernel at each trial mass. In `fixed-physical` mode it passes `mu * rms_fm` to `practise` and `mu * R_fm` to the weak kernel, so the dimensionless code radius obeys `R_code(mu) = mu R_code(1)` for a fixed physical nuclear size.

### 26.3 Implemented weak potential
The executable copied-tree weak source is a one-radius uniform hard sphere, not a Fermi distribution and not separate proton/neutron densities. In the active f2py path:

```text
R_rel = R_fm / electron_reduced_compton
source_factor = Cv_p Z - N
rho0 = (3 / (4 pi)) (1 / R_rel)^3 source_factor
       Gfermi_relativistic / (2 sqrt(2))
rho(r) = rho0, r <= R_rel
rho(r) = 0,    r > R_rel
V_W^pc(r) = Cv_e rho(r)
V_W^pv(r) = rho(r)
```

The factor `3/(4 pi R_rel^3)` is the normalized spatial hard-sphere profile. The stored `rho0` is not a unit-normalized density by itself; it already includes `Gfermi_relativistic/(2 sqrt(2))` and the implemented weak source factor `Cv_p Z - N`.

### 26.4 Coupling convention
The constants file implements

```text
sin2thetaW = 0.2312
Cv_e = 1 - 4 sin2thetaW = 0.0752
Cv_p = 1 - 4 sin2thetaW = 0.0752
```

The documentation comments in the active constants files have been aligned to these executable formulas. No numerical formula was changed: all current prototype numbers use `Cv_e = 1 - 4 sin2thetaW` and `Cv_p = 1 - 4 sin2thetaW`.

The implemented effective source factor is

```text
Q_impl = Cv_p Z - N.
```

For the copied-tree diagonal PC source, `Cv_e` is then multiplied separately:

```text
V_W^pc proportional to Cv_e (Cv_p Z - N).
```

Within the copied code this is not internally double-counted: `Cv_p` enters only through `Q_impl`, and `Cv_e` enters only in `eval_pc`. This executable convention is now the project convention for the internal common-radius prototype.

### 26.5 Unit convention
The active path uses relativistic units with `hbar = c = m_e = 1`, even though several variable names still carry legacy labels such as `R_au` or `rho0_au`.

The constants are:

```text
electron_reduced_compton = hbarc / electron_rest_energy
                         = 386.1592676580278 fm
Gfermi_relativistic = Gfermi * electron_rest_energy_GeV^2
                    = 3.0456471623406034e-12
```

Thus every weak-kernel radius conversion in the sampled-rad path is

```text
R_rel = R_fm / electron_reduced_compton.
```

For the mass derivative, varying a trial electron mass at fixed physical nuclear radius changes the dimensionless code radius as `R_code(mu) = mu R_code(1)`. The weak normalization also scales as `G_F m_e^2`. Because the wrapped Fortran kernel stores `Gfermi_relativistic` as a fixed built-in constant, `Mass_derivative.py` applies the missing trial-mass normalization externally as `mu^2`. This is mathematically consistent with the current `Gfermi_relativistic = G_F m_e^2` convention and does not change the sign of the weak source.

The source factor is negative because the neutron term dominates `Cv_p Z`. Therefore `rho0` is negative, and since `Cv_e` is positive in the implemented code, `V_W^pc` is also negative. For a normalized `1s` sampled density, `(G^2 + F^2)` is nonnegative, so the diagonal first-order `Delta E_W` is negative in this convention. ### 26.7 Copied-tree versus protected-tree status
The copied tree under `workspace_f2py` and the protected/original tree under `workspace` implement the same diagonal PC weak source for first-order energy shifts:

```text
V_W^pc = Cv_e rho(r).
```

The copied tree explicitly separates PC and PV weak sources in `weak_potential`: `eval_pc = Cv_e rho(r)` and `eval_pv = rho(r)`. The copied `rad` and `rad_f2py` weak off-diagonal matrix elements use the PV source without the extra `Cv_e` factor.

The protected tree has only one `potential%eval`, and that single evaluation returns `Cv_e rho(r)`. Therefore protected-tree off-diagonal weak/PV matrix elements use `Cv_e rho(r)` rather than `rho(r)`. This is a real copied/protected convention difference for parity-mixing calculations.

For the current PC-only sampled mass-derivative prototype, this copied/protected PV difference does not change the active `Delta E_W = integral (G^2 + F^2) V_W^pc dr` calculation. It must still be treated as a convention blocker before any APV, parity-mixing, or PV-amplitude result is used.

### 26.8 Remaining convention risks
Remaining issues before any Pb/U benchmark or production weak-`g` result:

- The implemented `Cv_e = Cv_p = 1 - 4 sin^2(theta_W)` formulas are frozen for the internal common-radius prototype, but a later production paper may still need a separate electroweak-convention note if the model is upgraded.
- The current source is a common-radius hard sphere. It is an internal model, not the final explicit proton-density plus neutron-density source needed for production.
- The current `Q_impl = Cv_p Z - N` convention is frozen for all current prototype numbers; it must be deliberately revisited only if the production paper adopts a different weak-charge definition.
- The copied/protected PV split is not identical and must be resolved before using parity-mixing amplitudes.
- Several names and comments still say atomic units even though the active sampled path uses relativistic units.
- The sampled-rad route remains a support path. A direct coefficient-basis/DKB weak-energy driver is still needed before production claims.

### 26.9 Decision
Current weak-source convention is frozen for internal common-radius prototype only.

## 33. Fermi-density reconnaissance

### 33.1 Purpose
The next physics upgrade after hard spheres is a weak source with separately normalized proton and neutron Fermi densities. This pass does not implement that source. It only records the existing Fermi-related infrastructure in `workspace_f2py` and identifies the safest future implementation path.

### 33.2 Existing Fermi-related code
Relevant files and routines found in the copied workspace are:

- `workspace_f2py/codes/bspqed/src/bspqed_potentials.f90`: defines `fermi_potential` and `fermi_potential_eval` for a finite-size Coulomb potential in the `bspqed` function hierarchy. The stored fields are `zalpha`, `a`, and `c`.
- `workspace_f2py/codes/bspwf/src/bspwf_potentials.f90`: defines the analogous `bspwf` `fermi_potential`, with fields `znucl`, `a`, and `c`.
- `workspace_f2py/programs/rad/src/practise.f90`: the SCF path uses `type(fermi_potential) :: nuclpot_scf` and initializes the Fermi charge potential in `initialize_scf_problem`.
- `workspace_f2py/programs/hartree/src/dhfcb.f90`: uses `bspwf_potentials::fermi_potential` for the Hartree-Fock nuclear potential.
- `workspace_f2py/programs/hartree_f2py/src/dhfcb_f2py.f90`: wraps a Hartree-Fock path that also uses `fermi_potential`.
- `workspace_f2py/codes/bspqed/src/bspqed_fourier_density.f90`: contains point and uniform-sphere Fourier densities only; no Fermi Fourier density was found.
- `workspace_f2py/programs/rad_f2py/src/rad_kernel_f2py.f90`: does not currently import or use `fermi_potential`; the active direct-DKB weak routes are hard-sphere routes.

No implemented weak Fermi density, Woods-Saxon weak density, symmetrized Fermi weak density, or separate proton/neutron 2pF weak source was found.

### 33.3 Existing parameter convention
The clearest existing parameter convention is in `workspace_f2py/programs/rad/src/practise.f90`, where the SCF nuclear Fermi parameters are computed in fm first:

```text
a_F = 2.3 / (4 ln 3) fm
c_F = sqrt((5 rms_fm^2 - 7 (pi a_F)^2) / 3) fm
```

This is equivalent to the usual two-parameter Fermi rms relation:

```text
rms^2 = (3/5) c_F^2 + (7/5) pi^2 a_F^2
```

The `practise.f90` SCF setup then converts to bohr for the `bspwf` Coulomb potential:

```text
a_code = a_F / bohr_radius
c_code = c_F / bohr_radius
```

The active direct-DKB weak-energy route instead uses relativistic lengths:

```text
r_rel = r_fm / electron_reduced_compton
```

Therefore a future direct-DKB weak Fermi density must convert `a_F` and `c_F` to relativistic code units, not bohr. The existing `fermi_potential_eval` routines include an analytic Coulomb potential normalization factor `n`, but they evaluate electrostatic potential, not the local density profile needed by `intlw`.

One caution: `workspace_f2py/programs/hartree_f2py/src/dhfcb_f2py.f90` appears to convert `afermi` to bohr before using it inside the `cfermi` rms formula with `rms`, and then divides `cfermi` by `bohr_radius` again. The fm-first `practise.f90` convention is the safer reference.

### 33.4 Reusable pieces
Reusable as references:

- the 2pF parameter convention in `practise.f90`;
- the analytic Coulomb-potential structure in `bspqed_potentials.f90` and `bspwf_potentials.f90`;
- the existing `function_type` interface pattern used by `intlw`.

Not directly reusable for weak densities:

- the existing `fermi_potential_eval` routines return Coulomb potentials, not normalized density values;
- the existing weak sources store hard-sphere normalizations and step-function profiles;
- the active direct-DKB weak route uses relativistic lengths, while the SCF Fermi Coulomb path uses bohr.

### 33.5 Proposed Fermi weak-source model
The intended future weak source is:

```text
rho_i(r) = rho0_i / [1 + exp((r - c_i)/a_i)],  i = p,n
```

with the proton and neutron densities normalized separately:

```text
4 pi integral_0^infinity r^2 rho_i(r) dr = 1
```

The future parity-conserving weak potential would be:

```text
V_W^pc(r) =
G_F/(2 sqrt(2)) Cv_e [Cv_p Z rho_p(r) - N rho_n(r)]
```

The density normalization constants `rho0_i` should be computed for the actual code-unit `a_i` and `c_i` used in the direct-DKB integral. This normalization must be validated explicitly before any neutron-skin study.

### 33.6 Proposed implementation strategy
The safest next implementation path is:

- first implement a common Fermi weak density with a single `a` and `c`;
- validate its density normalization independently;
- then implement separate proton/neutron Fermi densities;
- validate that equal proton/neutron Fermi parameters reproduce the common Fermi result;
- compare Fermi versus hard-sphere results for the same rms radius;
- then vary neutron skin.

The DKB Coulomb potential choice should be decided explicitly at that stage. A minimal first Fermi weak-source test could still use the frozen proton hard-sphere Coulomb potential to isolate the weak-density change, but a later production-style model would need a consistent Fermi charge potential for the Coulomb field.

### 33.7 Decision
Existing Fermi infrastructure is useful only as a reference and a new weak-density type is needed.

## 37. Independent Fermi Coulomb potential validation

### 37.1 Purpose
Section 36 made the proton-Fermi Coulomb direct-DKB route runnable, using the existing analytic `bspqed` Fermi Coulomb potential. Before this route can be frozen as the production-style Coulomb route, the analytic Coulomb evaluator must be checked against an independent Coulomb potential constructed directly from a normalized two-parameter Fermi charge density.

This section validates only the Coulomb potential. It does not change the frozen weak convention, the separate proton/neutron Fermi weak-density implementation, or any mass-derivative physics table.

### 37.2 Analytic code formula
The analytic evaluator is `bspqed_potentials::fermi_potential_eval` in:

```text
workspace_f2py/codes/bspqed/src/bspqed_potentials.f90
```

For positive `x`, the code sets:

```text
a -> a/c
r -> x/c
s3a = S_3(-1/a)
n = 1 + a^2 [pi^2 - 6 a S_3(-1/a)]
V(x) = -zalpha factor / (n x)
```

where `S_l` is the finite weighted series implemented by `sfunc(l,x)`. For `r < 1`,

```text
factor =
    6 a^3 [S_3((r - 1)/a) - S_3(-1/a)]
  + (1/2) r a^2 [pi^2 - 6 S_2((r - 1)/a)]
  + (1/2) r (3 - r^2).
```

For `r >= 1`,

```text
factor = n + 3 a^2 [r S_2((1 - r)/a) + 2 a S_3((1 - r)/a)].
```

The sign convention is the attractive electron potential: callers set `zalpha = Z alpha`, so the potential is negative. The unit convention is relativistic direct-DKB units: `c_rel = c_fm / electron_reduced_compton`, `a_rel = a_fm / electron_reduced_compton`, and the strength is `Z alpha`.

At exactly `x = 0`, the routine returns:

```text
V(0) = 0.
```

This is a special code branch and is not the finite physical central Coulomb potential. For large `x`, the `S_2` and `S_3` terms in the exterior branch decay exponentially, `factor -> n`, and therefore:

```text
V(x) -> -Z alpha / x.
```

Thus the analytic expression is normalized to the total Coulomb tail for positive radii.

### 37.3 Independent numerical reference
The independent reference uses the normalized spherical proton charge density:

```text
rho_p(r) = rho0 / [1 + exp((r - c)/a)]
4 pi integral_0^infinity r^2 rho_p(r) dr = 1.
```

The finite-size Coulomb potential is evaluated as:

```text
V_C(r) =
-Z alpha [
    Q_inside(r)/r
  + tail_integral(r)
]
```

with:

```text
Q_inside(r) = integral_0^r 4 pi r'^2 rho_p(r') dr'
tail_integral(r) = integral_r^infinity 4 pi r' rho_p(r') dr'.
```

At the origin, the limiting value is:

```text
V_C(0) = -Z alpha integral_0^infinity 4 pi r rho_p(r) dr.
```

The numerical reference is implemented in:

```text
workspace_f2py/Validate_fermi_coulomb.py
```

It constructs and normalizes the density directly, then integrates the Coulomb formula with numerical quadrature. It does not use `fermi_potential_eval` for the reference values.

# 42. Direct magnetic-response sign check

This is a sign/prefactor diagnostic only. It does not use the weak source, does not change the frozen weak convention, does not change the p/n Fermi final route, and does not update the final Pb/U neutron-skin table.

### 42.2 Test potential
The test perturbation is a controlled non-weak scalar potential:

```text
V_test(r; mu) = lambda_code(mu) exp[-r / R0_code(mu)].
```

It was added only as a diagnostic potential:

```text
bspqed_potentials::exponential_test_potential
```

with no weak constants, no Coulomb charge factor, and no hidden angular prefactor. The sign is controlled entirely by `lambda_code`.

The fixed-physical-potential convention used for the mass derivative is:

```text
R0_code(mu) = mu R0_code(1)
lambda_code(mu) = lambda_code(1) / mu.
```

Thus the range is held fixed in physical length units, while the dimensionless code amplitude is scaled as a fixed physical energy divided by the trial `m_e c^2` unit. No weak-like `mu^2` factor is applied in this diagnostic.

The numerical diagnostic used:

```text

```

The smaller `npoints` value is deliberate: this diagnostic requires an explicit first-order perturbed wavefunction sum over the DKB spectrum and is not a production convergence scan.

### 42.3 Direct magnetic-response route
The direct route follows the structure of `workspace_f2py/programs/gfactor/src/g_uehling.f90`, but replaces the Uehling potential by `V_test` and removes the Uehling-specific normalization factor.

The Fortran diagnostic wrapper is:

```text
rad_kernel_f2py::direct_dkb_exponential_test_response_1s
```

For each input it:

1. solves the unperturbed finite-sphere Coulomb H-like `1s` DKB problem;
2. selects the `1s` state using the same positive-spectrum index convention as the direct-DKB weak route;
3. computes the scalar energy shift:

```text
Delta_E_test = <a|V_test|a> = intlw(..., V_test, ...);
```

4. computes the first-order perturbed coefficients:

```text
call perturbation(..., V_test, ..., delta_coefs);
```

5. evaluates the magnetic matrix element with the existing repository kernel:

```text
delta_g_direct = 2 gfactor(..., coefa, delta_coefs, linear_potential, ...).
```

The factor of two is the same bra-plus-ket first-order cross-term factor used by `g_uehling.f90`. No factor `(alpha/pi) Z alpha` is applied, because that belongs to the Uehling model normalization and not to this raw scalar diagnostic.

### 42.4 Mass-derivative route for V_test
For the same scalar test potential, the mass-derivative branch computes:

```text

[
    (1+h) Delta_epsilon_test(1+h)
```

Here `Delta_epsilon_test(mu)` is the raw code-unit scalar energy shift at trial `mu`, with:

```text
rms_eff(mu) = mu rms_fm

lambda_eff(mu) = lambda(mu=1) / mu.
```

The two candidate mass-derivative predictions are then:

